    Cyber Academy

    Six habits that stop most attacks before they work.

    Tools catch what they're built to catch. These are the instincts that catch everything else. Tap a card to open it.

    Beginner

    Spot a phishing email

    The four tells that give away a fake message, even a well-written one.

    Open module
    • Check the sender's actual address, not just the display name.
    • Urgency is a tactic — "act now or lose access" is designed to stop you thinking.
    • Hover any link before clicking to see where it really goes.
    • Real companies rarely ask for passwords or OTPs by email.
    Beginner

    Build a password you'll actually remember

    Passphrases beat "P@ssw0rd1" — here's how to make one that's both strong and memorable.

    Open module
    • Four random unrelated words beat eight random characters.
    • Length matters more than complexity — aim for 14+ characters.
    • Never reuse a password across two important accounts.
    • Let a password manager remember the ones you can't.
    Essential

    Turn on two-factor authentication

    Why one extra step blocks the vast majority of account takeovers.

    Open module
    • An authenticator app is safer than SMS codes, which can be intercepted.
    • Start with email, banking, and any account tied to password resets.
    • Save backup codes somewhere offline, not in your inbox.
    • A leaked password alone can't get in once 2FA is on.
    Intermediate

    Public Wi-Fi: what's actually risky

    Less about the coffee-shop network, more about what you do on it.

    Open module
    • Most sites use HTTPS now, which limits what others on the network can see.
    • Avoid logging into banking or sensitive accounts on shared networks.
    • A VPN helps, but a fake "free VPN" app can be worse than no VPN.
    • Turn off auto-connect to open networks you don't recognize.
    Intermediate

    Social engineering tactics to know

    Most breaches start with a human being convinced, not a system being hacked.

    Open module
    • Pretexting: a caller invents a believable reason to ask for information.
    • Authority pressure: "this is IT, I need your password to fix it now."
    • Baiting: a free USB drive or download that's actually malware.
    • Verify unexpected requests through a second, known channel first.
    Essential

    Software updates and why they matter

    That "remind me later" is often standing between you and a known exploit.

    Open module
    • Most updates patch security flaws that are already public knowledge.
    • Attackers actively scan for devices still running old versions.
    • Turn on automatic updates for your OS, browser, and apps.
    • Uninstall apps you no longer use — they still carry risk.
